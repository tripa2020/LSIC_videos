# Building Generalist Robots with Foundation Models and Internet-Scale Data

## Summary
This talk explores the paradigm shift in robotics from manually coded behaviors to learning-based approaches using foundation models. The speakers from Physical Intelligence explain how combining pre-trained vision-language models with robotics data allows robots to leverage internet-scale knowledge for physical tasks. They detail their progress on capability and generalization, identifying performance and robustness as the primary remaining challenge. The discussion covers novel methods like 'knowledge insulation' to prevent catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning and concludes with the prediction that general-purpose home robots will be deployed within 5-10 years.

## Operating Algorithm
Find the true bottleneck via existence proofs (teleop shows hardware is solved → intelligence is the constraint) → decompose the remaining problem into ordered axes (capability → generalization → performance) and attack them strictly in sequence → choose tasks purely for information-per-week (hard enough to be a proof, familiar enough to self-evaluate, no customer dependency) → read weak-but-generalizing signals in 'pathetic' demos as stronger evidence than polished demos → control eval variance with paired same-day baselines before trusting any number → hunt for a repeatable *recipe* (even as crude as dollars→capability) that converts an open research problem into an execution problem → when reality beats your prior (100 homes, not a million), update the model generating the prior, not just the estimate

## Key Points
- The field of robotics has fundamentally shifted from manual programming to a learning-based approach where robots learn from experience. `[04:08]`
- A key breakthrough is combining large, pre-trained vision-language models with smaller robotics datasets, transferring internet-scale knowledge to physical actions. `[07:15]`
- After major progress in capability and generalization, the primary challenge for robotics foundation models is now performance, specifically robustness and success rate. `[10:04]`
- The current bottleneck in robotics is intelligence and software, not hardware, as demonstrated by human teleoperation of existing robots. `[15:19]`
- Open-sourcing models is a deliberate strategy based on the belief that the biggest risk to the field is collective scientific failure, not competition. `[48:45]`
- Fine-tuning vision-language models on robotics data can cause them to lose general capabilities, a problem addressed by a technique called 'knowledge insulation'. `[50:52]`
- The ultimate goal is to create generalist, promptable base models for robotics that require little to no task-specific post-training, similar to modern LLMs. `[55:58]`

## Methods / Approach
- Knowledge Insulation: A training recipe to adapt VLMs for robotics without losing general capabilities by co-training on web data and robotics data. `[50:52]`
- Tokenizing robot actions into a text-like format to adapt the VLM backbone to robotics in a way that preserves its existing knowledge. `[50:53]`
- Applying a technique analogous to image in-painting to action trajectories to compensate for model inference latency and ensure smooth robot motion. `[55:57]`
- Using large-scale teleoperation to collect diverse, real-world data for training robot policies. `[40:41]`
- Evaluating new models by running them concurrently with a baseline model to compare relative performance in non-resettable physical scenes. `[36:36]`

## How to Learn It (So It Sticks)
**First-order terms:** VLA / robot foundation model (a vision-language model pre-trained on internet data, then adapted with a comparatively small amount of robot data so web-scale concepts connect to motor actions — the RT-2 'move the coke can to Taylor Swift' insight) · capability→generalization→performance ladder (PI's three-axis roadmap: do hard dexterous tasks at all, then work in unseen environments, then work reliably enough to deploy — the third rung is unsolved) · knowledge insulation (training recipe: backbone learns from tokenized text-like actions plus web data while a gradient-stopped flow-matching action head produces motions, preserving internet knowledge and yielding a 10x training speedup) · action chunk (a predicted trajectory of many future actions rather than a single step, which is what makes in-painting-based real-time control possible) · paired same-session evaluation (running new policy and baseline simultaneously with the same operator and trusting only relative ranking, because scenes can never be reset identically) · teleoperation existence proof (if a human teleoperating a robot body can do the task, hardware is sufficient and the bottleneck is intelligence) · dollars-to-capability scaling law (a crude monotonic curve from money spent — on data, compute, whatever — to model capability; possessing it would convert robotics from a research problem into an execution problem) · task-hardness taxonomy (a task is hard for robots in proportion to initial-state variance, horizon length where errors compound, and precision bottlenecks — laundry from a basket maximizes the first)

- Q: What existence proof does Hausman use to argue robotics is bottlenecked on intelligence rather than hardware, and what is the general rule it yields? `[15:18]`
  A: The PR1 demo: a 10+-year-old robot tidying a home impressively — but teleoperated by a human. Since a human brain driving the same body succeeds, the hardware is provably sufficient; rule: anything achievable via teleoperation proves the remaining deficit is entirely software/intelligence.
- Q: In the thought experiment where you delete all humans and other cars from the world, what asymmetry between autonomous driving and robot manipulation is exposed? `[14:41]`
  A: Driving becomes rather trivial (a car never has to touch anything), while manipulation stays just as hard because touching the world is intrinsic to the task — 'contacts make things exponentially hard.' Only the long-tail-of-rare-events lesson transfers between the two fields.
- Q: How many training homes did it actually take for Pi-0.5 to generalize to an unseen home, versus the team's pre-project estimate — and what worldview update did Hausman draw from the gap? `[61:27]`
  A: About 100 homes versus pre-project guesses of ~a million. The update: the world's effective diversity (as seen by these models) is far lower than human intuition suggests — 'we're giving too much credit to how diverse the world is.'
- Q: Explain the mechanism of 'knowledge insulation' and the two results it produced. `[50:52]`
  A: Fine-tuning a vision-language backbone with diffusion/flow-matching action gradients destroys its internet-learned features, so PI instead trains the backbone on tokenized (text-like) actions plus web data, then bolts a flow-matching action expert on top with stopped gradients so the two barely communicate. Results: ~10x faster training and better generalization.
- Q: Since robot scenes can never be reset identically, how does PI decide whether a new policy is actually better than the old one? `[36:34]`
  A: They never compare absolute scores across days; they run the new model and the baseline at the same time, with the same operator (rating against written guidelines), across many tasks and environments, and trust only the statistically-tested relative ranking.
- Q: Of PI's three research axes (capability, generalization, performance), which remains unsolved as of this talk, and why does Hausman say it's qualitatively different? `[12:02]`
  A: Performance/robustness. Unlike capability (solved via Pi-0's dexterous tasks) and generalization (Pi-0.5 in unseen homes), there's no clear 'just add more data or diversity' path — it needs new algorithmic ideas; current models are 'demo ready,' not 'deployment ready.'
- Q: What is the 'first gate' Hausman says simulation must pass before being trusted for anything bigger, and what mechanism explains why sim worked for locomotion but not manipulation? `[42:03]`
  A: Simulation must first become an accepted evaluation tool (the cheap, low-stakes use) before being trusted as a training data source. Locomotion only requires modeling your own body (legs), which sim does well; manipulation requires modeling everything the arm touches, which is far harder and less scalable.
- Q: How did Kevin Black's real-time inference fix work, and from what unrelated field was the technique transplanted? `[55:56]`
  A: From image in-painting in diffusion models: since inference takes a few hundred milliseconds while the robot keeps moving, the already-executed portion of the action chunk (a predicted trajectory segment) is treated as the 'known patch' and the model in-paints a consistent continuation — purely algorithmic, requiring no retraining.

## Field Implications — Where to Steer
- Practitioners must gain expertise in training and fine-tuning large, multi-modal foundation models, moving beyond traditional robotics control algorithms. `[04:08]`
- Develop skills in large-scale data collection, curation, and management, as diverse, real-world data is the primary driver of model generalization. `[40:41]`
- Shift research and development focus toward improving model robustness and success rates, as this is now the main barrier to deployment. `[10:04]`

## Industry Outlook — Fading vs Thriving
**📉 Fading**
- Manually coding specific robot behaviors and control policies for every task. `[04:08]`
- The belief that robotics is primarily a hardware-limited field. `[15:19]`
- Focusing on a single robot form factor (e.g., humanoids) as the only path to general intelligence. `[63:40]`

**📈 Thriving**
- Learning-based approaches using foundation models (VLMs) trained on internet-scale and robotics data. `[07:15]`
- General-purpose, promptable robot base models applicable to various tasks with minimal fine-tuning. `[55:58]`
- Open-source collaboration on base models to accelerate scientific progress across the entire field. `[48:45]`
